1Problem

So the problem I was working on was predicting whether a loan applicant is going to default. One main table with a row per applicant, and six history tables behind it — bureau records, previous applications, payment history — with many rows per person. Only 8% of people default, and the metric is AUC, so this is really a ranking problem — "everyone repays" gets 92% accuracy and a useless model, so I stopped looking at accuracy entirely.

2Read the data

Before I built anything, I spent time just looking at the data, and that changed how I cleaned it. The employment-days column had this value 365243 — that's about a thousand years — in 55,000 rows, and they were all pensioners. So it's a placeholder for "no employment date". I blanked it out and kept a flag, and that column went from hurting the model to a top-5 feature. I also found that missing values themselves predict default, so I made a deliberate call: I didn't impute or drop anything. The textbook approach — drop sparse columns, fill the rest — would've deleted one of my strongest features. Gradient boosting reads NaN natively, so the missingness just becomes signal.

3Features

This is where most of the score came from. I wrote one helper that collapses each history table down to one row per applicant — means, maxes, counts, category proportions — and I added the tables one at a time so I could see exactly what each one was worth. Then I added nine domain ratios — credit-to-income, payment-to-income, credit-to-goods — and a combined mean of the three external credit scores, which ended up being my number one feature. All of that together took me from 0.7586 to 0.7863. One thing I like to point out: one of those ratios had basically zero correlation with the target and still finished top-6 by gain. So my rule became — correlation is a screen, cross-validation is the judge.

4Cut noise

At that point I had 747 features, and I knew I couldn't trust raw importance at that scale, because noise features score by luck. So I used null-importance selection — shuffle the target, retrain 30 times, and only keep a feature if its real importance beats its own noise distribution. That took me from 747 down to 159, and the score actually went up. Almost 80% of what I'd engineered was noise. Smaller model, faster, less overfit.

5Honest CV

Quick word on how I measured all this, because it matters for what comes next. Everything is 5-fold stratified out-of-fold AUC — every row gets predicted once by a model that never saw it. I kept the folds fixed and shared across every model, so all the predictions line up row-for-row — that's what makes stacking possible.

6Diversity

Then on the same 159 features I trained XGBoost, CatBoost, and a PyTorch neural net, all through one generic CV runner. CatBoost came out as my best single model at 0.7904. The neural net was the weakest — but it's the only non-tree model, so it makes different mistakes, and that's exactly why I included it. It got its own preprocessing pipeline, fit on the training fold only.

7Failed stack

Here's the part I find most interesting. I stacked the four models with a logistic-regression meta-learner, and it lost — it scored worse than CatBoost by itself. When I dug into why: logistic regression minimizes log-loss, but I'm being scored on AUC. My models were outputting probabilities on different calibration scales, so the meta-learner was busy fixing calibration and wrecking the ranking. The fix was to stack on ranks instead of probabilities — ranks are scale-invariant, and ranking is literally what AUC measures. That took me to 0.7916. And one more thing I found: if you pick weights to maximize AUC directly, the neural net gets zeroed out. Diversity only helps if the model is also strong enough.

8Takeaway

Reading the journey back, it's a ranking of what actually moves the score: features, then feature selection, then model choice, and ensembling last. A single LightGBM got me 95% of the way. The ensemble was the last 0.001. And I tuned nothing, so there's another couple of thousandths still on the table.

Weak links — ready answers

Where a sharp interviewer will push, and what I say. The two in red need the most rehearsal.

Rigor — the ones that can hurt

How much do you trust 0.7916?

As a comparison number between my own variants, fully. As a generalization estimate, it's slightly optimistic — I have no untouched holdout, my feature selection ran on all the training rows, and 0.7916 is the best of five stacking variants scored on the same OOF set. The honest fix is nesting selection inside each fold and holding out a slice before anything touches it. I'd expect the true number to be a few thousandths lower, not the ranking of decisions to change.

Your best model is CatBoost. Why deploy XGBoost?

Because deployment is a container question, not just an AUC question. The difference was 0.0006. A prebuilt SageMaker XGBoost image matched my local version exactly, so the model file loads natively — no custom container, no dependency pinning. CatBoost would have meant bring-your-own-container for six ten-thousandths.

Why didn't you deploy the ensemble?

Because the rank-stack isn't servable as written. It ranks each model's predictions across the batch — at batch size one, every model ranks 1.0 and the meta-learner outputs a constant. Making it real-time means storing an empirical CDF per base model and re-validating, all for +0.0012 over one model. That's the real argument for shipping a single model — and I found it by trying to deploy.

This is a credit model — what about fairness?

I didn't do a fairness analysis, and I'd want to before this went near a real decision. The data includes gender and age, and the model uses them. The next step is per-group AUC and calibration, then dropping or constraining protected attributes and measuring the cost. As a Kaggle-style project the scope was predictive rigor; as a lending product, that's not sufficient.

Design choices

Why LightGBM as the baseline?

It consumes NaN and categoricals natively, so my EDA decisions — keep the missingness, don't one-hot — go straight in without a preprocessing layer that could leak. It's also fast enough to re-run 5-fold CV after every table I added, which is what let me attribute each gain.

Only 8% positives — why no class weighting or SMOTE?

Because the metric is AUC — threshold-free, scored on ranking. Resampling and class weights mostly shift calibration and the decision threshold; they don't much change how a tree ensemble orders applicants. I stratified the folds and early-stopped on AUC. If the target were a threshold metric like F1 or a cost-weighted decision, I'd revisit it.

Why the 75th percentile for null-importance?

I checked 50, 75 and 90 and looked at how many features survived each. 75 kept a set that held the score under CV — and that's the real test. The percentile is just a knob; cross-validation decides.

The neural net was weak — did it actually matter?

Barely. AUC-optimal weights zero it out, and in a later ablation the whole non-tree group was worth 0.00005 — inside noise. I keep it in the story because it's the clearest demonstration that diversity only pays when the model is also strong. It's not in the working stack.

Scope

What didn't work?

Two things. The raw-probability stack. And a later experiment where I added logistic regression and Naive Bayes for more diversity — Naive Bayes was four times more decorrelated from the trees than anything else, and it still contributed nothing; removing it improved the stack. That killed the "more diverse models" direction and pointed the remaining headroom back at features.

What are the EXT_SOURCE features?

Anonymized external credit scores — the competition doesn't say from where. They're the strongest signal by far; my combined mean of the three was the #1 feature. It's worth knowing your best feature is a black box you didn't build.

Where would you go next?

Features, not models. One table — the monthly bureau balance — I never aggregated; it needs a two-level roll-up. And a proper holdout, so I can price the biases I just described.
